This is a simulated response from an experienced office manager and bookkeeper. The frequencies are representative estimates for the scenario, not measured statistics or claims of firsthand experience.
1. Why I would laugh at this plan
You're assuming the technicians fill out their paperwork correctly and the suppliers know what they're billing us for. Half the problem with my job isn't typing information into Excel; it's figuring out what actually happened. A perfectly readable document can be completely wrong, and your system might accept it without questioning a thing.
2. The 10 most important situations you're missing
Situation: 1. Work orders that never reach the office.
What the paperwork actually looks like: Nothing. The technician left it in the truck, lost it, forgot to photograph it, or thought another technician submitted it.
What should happen to it: Identify missing ticket numbers, reconcile them against scheduled jobs, and distinguish missing tickets from legitimately voided tickets.
How often you see it (weekly, monthly, rarely): Weekly.
What it costs if it's handled wrong: An entire job goes unbilled, including labor and materials.

Situation: 2. Incorrect information that looks perfectly legitimate.
What the paperwork actually looks like: A technician's handwritten 1.5 hours looks like 7.5 hours. A quantity of 12 looks like 17. A part number is off by one digit but happens to match another real part.
What should happen to it: Check against the original paperwork, technician time records, parts records and reasonable job-specific expectations.
How often you see it (weekly, monthly, rarely): Weekly.
What it costs if it's handled wrong: Overbilling, underbilling, incorrect payroll, inventory discrepancies and customer disputes.

Situation: 3. The customer, property owner and person responsible for payment are different.
What the paperwork actually looks like: The technician writes down the tenant's name and apartment address. The property management company is actually responsible for paying and requires a purchase order number.
What should happen to it: Identify the correct billing account, service location and any customer-specific billing requirements.
How often you see it (weekly, monthly, rarely): Weekly.
What it costs if it's handled wrong: Rejected invoices, delayed payments and time spent chasing the wrong people.

Situation: 4. Multiple visits or technicians working on the same job.
What the paperwork actually looks like: One technician diagnoses the problem Monday, another installs a replacement Wednesday, and someone returns Friday to finish the work. There are three tickets, and some materials or labor may be recorded more than once.
What should happen to it: Associate all tickets with the same job, reconcile overlapping entries and determine whether the job is ready for billing.
How often you see it (weekly, monthly, rarely): Weekly.
What it costs if it's handled wrong: Duplicate charges, missed charges, premature billing and inaccurate job profitability.

Situation: 5. Supplier invoices containing materials for multiple jobs.
What the paperwork actually looks like: A single invoice contains a water heater for one customer, fittings for another customer, and several boxes of supplies for truck inventory. The technician might have written a customer's name beside only one item.
What should happen to it: Allocate individual invoice lines and associated costs to the appropriate jobs, trucks or general inventory.
How often you see it (weekly, monthly, rarely): Weekly.
What it costs if it's handled wrong: Incorrect job costs, unbilled materials and misleading profit margins.

Situation: 6. Corrected or supplemented paperwork.
What the paperwork actually looks like: A technician submits a work order and later sends another photograph of the same ticket with additional parts or corrected hours. A supplier sends a revised invoice using the original invoice number.
What should happen to it: Recognize that the document has changed, retain the original version, identify the differences and have someone approve the correction.
How often you see it (weekly, monthly, rarely): Weekly.
What it costs if it's handled wrong: Your duplicate prevention rule throws away legitimate corrections, or both versions are entered and counted.

Situation: 7. Returns, supplier credits and warranty replacements.
What the paperwork actually looks like: A credit memo resembles an invoice. The original invoice reference may be buried in the description. A returned part might have a restocking fee, or a warranty replacement might have no material charge but still include freight.
What should happen to it: Identify the transaction as a credit or adjustment, associate it with the original purchase and correct the appropriate job or inventory costs.
How often you see it (weekly, monthly, rarely): Weekly.
What it costs if it's handled wrong: Paying suppliers too much, missing credits and overstating expenses.

Situation: 8. Paperwork that looks like an invoice but isn't payable.
What the paperwork actually looks like: Supplier statements, packing slips, order confirmations, quotations and delivery receipts containing supplier names, reference numbers, dates, prices and sometimes totals.
What should happen to it: Distinguish these document types from actual invoices. Use them for reconciliation or verification without recording them as additional amounts owed.
How often you see it (weekly, monthly, rarely): Weekly.
What it costs if it's handled wrong: Duplicate liabilities, potential duplicate payments and incorrect accounts payable balances.

Situation: 9. Warranty calls, callbacks and nonbillable work.
What the paperwork actually looks like: A normal work order with three hours of labor and replacement parts. Somewhere in the margin the technician writes “callback,” “warranty,” “no charge,” or “talk to owner.” Sometimes they write nothing.
What should happen to it: Determine who is responsible for the costs, whether the customer should be billed and whether a manufacturer warranty reimbursement is available. Record the actual costs regardless of billability.
How often you see it (weekly, monthly, rarely): Weekly.
What it costs if it's handled wrong: Charging customers for covered repairs, losing warranty reimbursements and understating the cost of callbacks.

Situation: 10. Incomplete documents and complicated invoice layouts.
What the paperwork actually looks like: A three-page invoice with totals on the final page, a photograph missing the bottom of a work order, notes on the reverse side, or an invoice containing freight charges, discounts, deposits, core charges and different units of measure. Every individual page may be perfectly readable.
What should happen to it: Verify document completeness, combine related pages, distinguish subtotals from final totals and account for all charges and adjustments.
How often you see it (weekly, monthly, rarely): Weekly.
What it costs if it's handled wrong: Missing expenses, incorrect quantities, incorrect tax calculations and inaccurate supplier balances.

Fields I would never manually enter into Excel
- Customer signature: I need to know whether the document was signed, but I don't need a transcription of the signature. Keep the original image.
- Full handwritten work description in a bookkeeping ledger: I need the description for customer billing and service history, but I don't need a wall of text in my financial records.
Everything else you've listed has a legitimate purpose somewhere in the business.
4. What one thing would make me refuse to use this system?
Silent failures.

If I cannot account for every document received, including documents the AI rejected, classified incorrectly, considered duplicates or failed to process, I won't use it.

I can deal with an exceptions list containing twenty problems. What I cannot deal with is an exceptions list containing five problems when there are actually twenty.

An error I know about is work. An error I don't know about is lost money.
